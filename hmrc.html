Industrial Placement · 2025/26 GOV.UK Service Design

I designed clearer ways for a large-scale public service to keep the people who rely on it informed.

Overview

Public services should feel effortless, even when the systems behind them aren't.

Over a nine-month placement I led a strategy workstream end to end, turning research into service improvements and interaction patterns aligned to GOV.UK standards, working across service, content and interaction design.

What I did

Notification Strategy

Designing a Notification Strategy Framework

A proposal for a coordinated, user-centred way for a large public service to communicate with the people who rely on it. New to the organisation, I led this, from understanding the ecosystem, to building the evidence and a shared design artefact, to pitching a new approach to stakeholders.

The brief

I joined the strategy team as an Associate Service Designer. With several projects running in parallel and this one just kicking off, my team lead asked me to lead the creation of a strategy end to end. This wasn't for a single service. It spanned several communication channels across a number of HMRC's high-volume citizen-facing services, so it was less a service and more of an ecosystem that needed changing. I would build the proposal in three months, drawing on the expertise of the people around me at every stage:

Senior Service Designers Project Managers Content Designers Interaction Designers

01 · Discover

Understand the ecosystem

Being new to the organisation, my first job was to understand the service as it exists today. This happened through many meetings, conversations and discussions with senior colleagues in my team and neighbouring teams, and with subject-matter experts who had shaped parts of the service before.

What does “good” even look like for a service I'd only just met?

These conversations surfaced how the current-state journey actually works, and the signals the service already produces, the evidence I'd lean on for everything that followed.

02 · Define

Establish the need

Was the pain point severe enough to justify a whole new strategic framework, or could it be solved with a few incremental changes?

I returned to the data to size the problem and answer exactly that. A new framework proposes an entirely new way for the system to operate, so the evidence had to justify that scale of change, not a tweak, but a rethink.

03 · Design

Choose the medium & build the map

With a colleague, I chose a service (ecosystem) map as the artefact to hold the strategy, and studied an international precedent closely to learn its structure, layers and elements.

We then built our own, gradually integrating the service's elements. I reviewed existing touchpoints to gauge the range and volume of user interactions, categorised them, and expressed the recurring interaction patterns visually within the map.

04 · Communicate

Create a key to read the map

A map only creates value if others can use it. So I created a key, a guide that walks through its layers, its slices and the elements inside, so anyone across the teams could read the map, interpret it, and make changes.

In parallel I partnered with content designers to shape the strategy narrative, its guiding principles and classification rules, and folded these into the deck.

05 · Pitch

Pitch & prototype the vision to stakeholders

With my team lead and senior service designers, I built the stakeholder pitch, framing the problem, the case for change, and the vision.

What does an effortless, connected experience actually feel like?

To make it tangible, I used a familiar everyday-app analogy: how a well-designed service handles the “unhappy path”, proactively updating and reassuring people when something goes wrong, and how that recovery builds trust.

Example, turning an “unhappy path” into trust

When a step breaks, a well-designed system proactively informs and reassures, turning a moment of uncertainty into one of trust. I contrasted this with where our own service was already strong, and where the gaps were.

I then brought the analogy back to a domain-relevant scenario and prototyped screens showing an ideal, reassuring end-to-end journey, the prototype was part of the pitch itself.

06 · Measure

Define the target

Finally, I grounded the proposal in a measurable goal. I worked through the existing evidence on where and why people currently need to get in touch.

From these, we chose a measurable target that reflected the lack of cohesion and usability in the current system, a reduction that was achievable, significant, and clearly worth pursuing.

The goal: a meaningful, measurable reduction in avoidable contact, the calls and queries that only happen because the system leaves people uncertain about what's happening and what to do next.

Service Design, Blueprint

Designing a Future-State Service Blueprint

The brief

My brief was to design an ideal future-state service blueprint for a major UK government life-event service, grounded in primary user research that surfaces the clearest opportunities for intervention and change against the current journey. It was a collaborative project, and I worked closely with:

User Researchers Service Designers Stakeholders
Current state

A journey scattered across the system

The service existed as scattered journey artefacts and stakeholder knowledge, spread across several parts of government. Mapping it end to end made the hand-offs, gaps and points of friction visible in one shared view.

Future / ideal state

A coherent, evidence-backed blueprint

A single future-state blueprint, grounded in research, user behaviour and need. It gives stakeholders an evidenced target to design towards, with clearly prioritised areas of opportunity and change.

My process

01 · Map

Mapping the current-state blueprint

As service designers, we started by mapping the information we already had about the current journey, drawing on stakeholder knowledge, subject-matter expertise and existing documentation, and mainly analysing the current user journeys they already had.

We mapped the current state in parallel with the user research interviews, so each stream informed the other as it developed. Scattered journey artefacts were drawn together into a single shared reference model the wider team could interrogate, and the gaps, assumptions and unknowns were made explicit so they could be tested in research.

02 · Discover

In-depth user interviews

Working with our user researchers, we ran a programme of in-depth, one-to-one interviews with a deliberately varied, representative sample of users with a range of experiences of the service.

My contribution was to take detailed notes during the sessions and then analyse them alongside senior user researchers, using structured mapping and analysis techniques to draw the patterns and meaning out of each interview.

In-depth, one-to-one interviews with a deliberately varied, representative sample of people who had lived through the journey.
03 · Analyse

Analysing the evidence

We brought the raw material of those conversations into a structured analysis, clustering individual observations into a consistent set of themes: needs, pain points, and the factors that shaped them.

Analysing the data this way turned a large, unstructured evidence base into a stable, defensible set of themes we could map back against the journey and carry forward with confidence.

06 · Validate

Validating with a second research round

We treated the first future-state blueprint as a hypothesis, framing clear problem hypotheses to test in a final round of research and validate what we believed to be true. Each insight we carried forward was anchored to supporting user quotes drawn directly from the research, so every claim the model made could be traced back to something a user had actually told us.

Where the evidence pushed back, we let it, confirming what held, discarding what did not, and sharpening the areas of opportunity and intervention rather than defending the first draft.

Round 2, validation research
07 · Analyse

Where user groups converge and diverge

With validated evidence in hand, we examined how different groups of users moved through the same journey. These groups were defined not by demographics but by differing mindsets and attitudes, and we mapped where their paths converged onto common ground and where they meaningfully diverged in approach and expectation across each stage.

This gave the blueprint the nuance to serve a varied population, without collapsing into a single, averaged user.

Varied starting pointsCommon groundDistinct needs
08 · Synthesise

Piecing together the final blueprint

Drawing together the findings from both rounds of research and our own analysis, we set out for each user group the common needs, pain points and stages they passed through, along with the actions that shaped how they moved through the journey. From these we produced a prioritised, research-backed list of areas and opportunities for improvement tailored to each group.

With these insights in hand, we pieced together the final future-state blueprint, drawing the evidence from our user research and the service design analysis into one model that showed the clearest opportunity areas both for each user group and for the life event as a whole.

The outcome was an evidence-backed model of the ideal journey, a shared artefact showing where the current journey falls short and where the greatest, most clearly prioritised opportunities to intervene and improve truly lie.

04, Workstream

International Research

The initiative

The programme was building an improved future-state blueprint for a major UK government life-event service. I led a comparative analysis of how other countries approach the same life event, so the team could take a genuinely global view: what tools each government provides at each stage to help users, which practices are already in place elsewhere, and what impact they have had, including how well citizens responded to them.

My process

01 · Initiate

Setting the scene before kickoff

This was a briefing for the team on how other countries approach designing for this life event. I brought together case studies, common practices, and the implementations and services that have been built successfully around it, so we could see how the problem is being solved internationally rather than only from our own vantage point.

The objective was for the team to learn from global practice: to consider and, where it fit, adopt approaches that had already proven themselves elsewhere, rather than reinventing the wheel.

Comparative briefing shared before kickoffPresented toWider teamLead service designer
02 · Research

A deep dive across comparable countries

For each country, I mapped the full set of government digital tools a person could encounter across the life event, and placed each one on the journey stage by stage, capturing both what it does for someone and where in the process it sits. The range was deliberately wide. At one end sat single-view portals that bring relevant information together in one place. Alongside these were interactive tools and plain-language guidance that help people understand their situation. Then came eligibility checkers, application services and step-by-step guidance that anyone can follow.

Because every tool was pinned to the same shared journey, I could compare provision like for like across all of them, instead of assessing each country in isolation.

Laid out this way, I could see how the approaches differ: at some stages a country supports people with genuinely helpful tools, while at others people are left to work it out alone.

03 · Synthesise

Key insights and patterns

The analysis catalogued the tools provided at each stage, identified which types of tool commanded the most engagement, isolated the features that set the strongest services apart, and ranked them from proactive to reactive.

Taken together, this gave the team a clear comparative evidence base to design from. It showed which kinds of tools and features actually get people engaging earlier rather than later, and it pinpointed a small number of high-impact features, single-view portals in particular, that were rare across these countries but clearly valuable wherever they did exist.

Ranking engagement, proactive to reactive

I analysed and ranked the countries on a proactive-to-reactive spectrum, judged by how actively citizens engage with the government services available to them.

What the ranking surfaced was that strong provision on its own does not create early engagement: the most proactive populations were those whose services actively prompted people to act, rather than waiting to be found.

05 · Deep-dive

A closer look at guided, personalised journeys

Guided, personalised journeys stood out: some countries offered them, others did not. I dug into how they work and briefed my lead service designer on how they give people a path tailored to their own situation, and on why users find that kind of experience so valuable.

Senior service designers recognised the analysis and pushed it forward in standups and weekly calls, seeing the value of carrying it into the later, more generative stages of the work such as ideation. It sharpened how we thought about the ideal-state experience, and positioned personalisation as a strong candidate for the future-state service.

06 · Impact

From research to direct knowledge-sharing

On the strength of the research, I was asked to narrow the focus to the countries most worth learning from, and that shortlist fed directly into senior leadership activity.

The work also travelled further than I expected. Senior leaders set up direct calls with the countries I had shortlisted, and our teams used them as genuine knowledge-sharing exchanges, comparing approaches and swapping documents and resources on both sides rather than trading summaries.

These conversations gave us a far broader perspective on how governments around the world approach the same problem. They helped us understand other countries' methodologies, the reasoning behind the decisions they had made, all of which fed directly into our ideation.
